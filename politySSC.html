Company Rule (1773–1947)

Regulating Act, 1773

Est. 1773

रेगुलेटिंग एक्ट — the first major step in the consolidation of British rule in India, tightening the British government's grip over a Company that had, until now, governed almost unchecked.

  • It was the first major step in the consolidation of British rule in India — strengthening British government control over the East India Company.
  • The Governor of Bengal was redesignated as the Governor-General of Bengal. Warren Hastings was the first Governor-General.
  • A Council of Four Members was created to assist the Governor-General.
  • A Supreme Court was set up: 1 Chief Justice + 3 other Judges. Elijah Impey was the first Chief Justice.
  • The Governors of the Madras and Bombay Presidencies were made subordinate to the Governor-General of Bengal.
  • Introduced changes in the Company's administration in two distinct areas: the Company's constitution at home and its territorial control in India.

Company Rule (1773–1947)

Pitt's India Act, 1784

Est. 1784

पिट्स इंडिया एक्ट — introduced Dual / Double Government: commerce stayed with the Company, but politics and administration passed to the British Crown.

Dual / Double Government

East India Company

Retained control through the Court of Directors (24 Members) over commercial activities & patronage (hiring).

British Government

Governed through the new Board of Control — handling political and administrative affairs.

Board of Control

  • A six-member Board of Control was established to supervise Indian affairs.
  • Members included: Secretary of State (Chairman), Chancellor of the Exchequer & four other members appointed by the Crown.
  • Powers: controlled all civil, military and revenue matters of the Company, and had access to all Company records and papers.

Structural & Administrative Changes

  • Bombay & Madras Presidencies were made subordinate to the Governor-General of Bengal — increasing centralisation.
  • British Possessions: the East India Company became subordinate to the British Government; for the first time, the Company's territories in India were officially called "British possessions."
  • Governor-General's Council: the number of members was reduced from four to three. The Commander-in-Chief was included in the Council.
  • Property Disclosure: civil and military officers of the Company were required to declare their property in India and Britain. Strict punishment was prescribed for corrupt officials.

Company Rule (1773–1947)

Charter Act of 1793

Est. 1793

चार्टर एक्ट, 1793 — renewed the Company's trade monopoly while quietly strengthening the Governor-General's authority and separating revenue from justice.

✔ Political Provisions

  • Royal approval became compulsory for appointing the Governor-General, Governors of Madras & Bombay, and the Commander-in-Chief.
  • When the Governor-General visited Madras or Bombay, he ranked above the Governors of those Presidencies.
  • If the Governor-General was absent from Bengal, he could appoint a Vice-President from among his Council members.

✔ Economic / Trade Provisions

  • Renewed the East India Company's trade monopoly for 20 years.
  • Increased financial burden on India — salaries paid from Indian revenues.
  • Allowed licensed private trade, expanding commercial activity.
  • Permitted Company dividends up to 10%.
  • Supported revenue extraction to fund British administrative costs.

Administrative Impact

  • The Governor-General received greater authority. The power earlier given only to Lord Cornwallis to override his Council was extended to all future Governors-General.
  • Control over Madras and Bombay was strengthened.
  • Separation of revenue and judicial functions improved administrative clarity.
  • The composition of the Board of Control was changed to: One President & Two junior members.

Governor-General under the Act

John Shore was the first Governor-General when the Act came into force. His authority became stronger in: Administration → Executive Control → Supervision over Madras & Bombay.

Judicial / Legal Provisions

  • The Act separated revenue administration from judicial functions.
  • District collectors and revenue officers could no longer act as judges.
  • Revenue courts known as Maal Adalats were abolished.
  • Higher courts at Calcutta continued: Sadar Diwani Adalat (civil appeals) and Sadar Nizamat Adalat (criminal appeals).
  • Provincial appellate courts continued at: Calcutta, Dacca, Murshidabad & Patna.

Company Rule (1773–1947)

1812 House of Commons Committee

Appt. 1808

A Select Committee appointed in 1808 by the British Parliament — its findings would shape the Charter Act of 1813.

Focus of the Inquiry

To examine the administration of the East India Company in India, primarily the judicial and police administration, which suffered from inefficiency and corruption.

Findings

The committee found weak control, corruption and mismanagement in these systems.

The Fifth Report & Its Legacy

  • The committee's findings were published in the famous "Fifth Report."
  • The Fifth Report strongly influenced the Charter Act of 1813.
  • It encouraged greater British government supervision over the East India Company.

Company Rule (1773–1947)

Charter Act, 1833

Est. 1833

A landmark act that completely changed the role of the East India Company — from trader to pure administrator of a unified India.

  • The East India Company's remaining trade monopoly was abolished — even trade with China and the tea trade ended.
  • The Governor-General of Bengal was redesignated as the Governor-General of India. Lord William Bentinck became the first Governor-General of India.
  • Legislative powers of Madras and Bombay Presidencies were taken away.
  • All law-making power was given to the Governor-General in Council.

Codification of Laws

  • The Act provided for the creation of the first Law Commission in India.
  • Lord Macaulay became its chairman — India's 4th (Law) Member of the Governor-General's Council was Thomas Babington Macaulay.
  • The Commission worked to codify Indian laws and later helped prepare the Indian Penal Code.

Inclusion of Indians in Services

  • The Act declared that no Indian should be disqualified from holding government office because of: Religion, Race, Caste, Place of Birth.

Administrative Importance

  • Made the administration of India more unified and centralised.
  • Marked the beginning of a modern system of administration under direct British control.

Charter Act, 1853

Est. 1853

The last of the Charter Acts — introduced open competition for the Civil Service and gave India its first mini-parliament.

J·E·L → Separation of Judicial, Executive & Legislative functions begins here

Crown Rule (1858–1947)

Government of India Act, 1858

Est. 1858

Also called the "Act for the Better Government of India" — the East India Company's rule ends; India becomes a direct British colony governed in the name of the Crown.

  • The Act abolished the rule of the East India Company and made India a direct British colony.
  • Indian territories were to be governed in the name of the British Queen (Victoria).
  • The system of dual government introduced by Pitt's India Act was abolished — the Board of Control and the Court of Directors were ended, effectively closing the double government system.
  • The powers of the Court of Directors were shifted to and vested in the Secretary of State for India (based in London). First Secretary of State for India: Lord Stanley.
  • The Secretary of State was assisted by a Council of 15 Members, which had only an advisory role. The Secretary of State was made Chairman of the Council.

Viceroy of India

  • The Governor-General was given the title of Viceroy, becoming the direct representative of the Crown. First Viceroy of India: Lord Canning.
  • All executive powers in India were concentrated in the hands of the Viceroy.
  • The Viceroy was assisted by an Executive Council.
  • Governors of Presidencies and the Viceroy were appointed by the Crown.

Doctrine of Lapse

The Act put an end to the Doctrine of Lapse. Indian princes were allowed to retain their kingdoms if they accepted British supremacy.

Pardon After the Mutiny

Pardon would be granted to all Indians who participated in the mutiny, except those who had killed British subjects.

Indian Civil Services & Queen's Proclamation

  • Provision for Indian Civil Services to administer the country, controlled by the Secretary of State.
  • Queen's Proclamation (1858) formally assured Indians that the British Crown would respect their customary religious and social practices and would not interfere in these matters.

✗ Defects of the Act

  • No representation was given to Indians in government.
  • Power remained completely in British hands.
  • The Secretary of State became the supreme authority over India.

✓ Importance

  • Ended East India Company rule.
  • Began direct Crown rule.
  • Strengthened imperial control over India — but did not make government more democratic or representative.

Crown Rule (1858–1947)

Indian Councils Act, 1861

Est. 1861

Introduced major administrative and legislative reforms — legislative power returns to Bombay and Madras, and Indians enter law-making for the first time.

  • Decentralisation: Legislative powers were restored to the Bombay and Madras Presidencies — reversing the centralising trend started by the Regulating Act and strengthened by the Charter Act. It later led to greater provincial autonomy.
  • New Legislative Councils were set up in: Bengal (1862), North-Western Frontier Province (1866) & Punjab (1897).
  • Beginning of Representative Institutions: Indians were associated with law-making for the first time — the first step toward representative government in India.
  • Nomination of Indians: The Viceroy was allowed to nominate Indians to the Legislative Council.
  • The first Indians nominated were: Raja of Benaras, Maharaja of Patiala & Sir Dinkar Rao.

Powers of the Viceroy & Limitations

Powers of the Viceroy

  • Could overrule the Council if necessary.
  • During emergencies, could issue ordinances without the Council's approval.
  • Such ordinances remained valid for 6 months.

Limitations

  • The Legislative Council had only an advisory role.
  • It could not discuss financial matters.
  • Indians had very little real power; the Governor-General still held absolute authority.
Crown Rule (1858–1947)

Indian Councils Act, 1892

Est. 1892

Legislative councils grow larger and gain the power to question government and discuss the budget — but still only through indirect nomination.

  • Nominations (indirect elections) were implemented.
  • The number of Legislative Councils was increased.
  • Legislative councils were given new responsibilities such as budget discussion and questioning the government.
  • The number of non-official members in Central and Provincial Legislative Councils was increased.

Crown Rule (1858–1947)

Indian Councils Act, 1909

Morley-Minto Reforms

The Morley–Minto Reforms expanded legislative councils and — controversially — introduced communal representation into Indian politics.

Expansion of Legislative Councils

  • The size of both Central and Provincial Legislative Councils was increased.
  • Central Legislative Council: membership increased to between 16 and 60 members.
  • Provincial Legislative Councils: Bengal Presidency – 50, Madras Presidency – 50, Bombay Presidency – 50, United Provinces – 50, Punjab – 30, Burma – 30 & Assam – 30 members.

Official & Non-Official Majority

  • In the Central Legislative Council, the official majority of government members was retained.
  • In the Provincial Legislative Councils, a non-official majority was introduced.
  • However, many non-official members were nominated rather than elected, so real control still remained with the government.

Indirect Election Process

  • Local bodies elected an electoral college.
  • The electoral college chose members of Provincial Legislative Councils.
  • Provincial councils then elected members to the Central Legislative Council.
  • Increased Indian Representation: the Act recognised the principle of election for non-official members; Indians were allowed to vote for legislative councils, based on class, property and community.
  • Indians in the Executive Council: for the first time, Indians were included in the Viceroy's Executive Council. Satyendra Prasanna Sinha became the first Indian member, appointed as Law Member.

Communal Representation — the Defining Feature

  • Legislative councils were given wider powers — members could ask supplementary questions, move resolutions on the budget, discuss matters of public interest, and vote on individual items of the budget. However, they could not vote on the budget as a whole.
  • Separate Electorates for Muslims: only Muslim voters could elect Muslim representatives.
  • Lower Qualification for Muslim Voters: income and property qualifications for Muslim voters were kept lower than those for Hindus.
  • The Act formally introduced communal representation into Indian politics. Therefore, Lord Minto is known as the "Father of the Communal Electorate."
  • Separate representation was also given to: Municipal corporations, Chambers of commerce, Universities & Zamindars.
Crown Rule (1858–1947)

Government of India Act, 1919

Montagu-Chelmsford

Also known as the Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms — India gets its first taste of Diarchy (dual government) at the provincial level.

Diarchy in Provinces

Diarchy means dual government — one part controlled by the British Governor, the other managed by Indian ministers. Provincial subjects were divided into Reserved and Transferred Subjects.

🛡 Reserved Subjects — Governor

  • Remained under control of the Governor and his Executive Council.
  • Included: Police, Prisons, Justice, Revenue, Forests, Press, Labour disputes, Irrigation & water, Agricultural loans.

🏛 Transferred Subjects — Indian Ministers

  • Handled by Indian ministers responsible to the legislature.
  • Included: Local self-government, Public works, Education, Sanitation, Industrial development, New industries.

Powers & Legislative Changes

  • The Governor-General and Secretary of State could interfere freely in reserved subjects; their interference in transferred subjects was limited.
  • No bill could become law without the approval of the Viceroy. The Viceroy could also pass a law without the approval of the legislature.
  • Bicameral Legislature: for the first time, a two-house legislature was introduced at the centre.

Rights of Legislators & Electoral Provisions

Rights of Legislators — Members could:

  • Ask questions
  • Move adjournment motions
  • Vote on some parts of the budget
  • But about 75% of the budget could not be voted upon.

Electoral Provisions

  • Separate electorates extended to: Muslims, Sikhs, Europeans & Anglo-Indians.
  • Limited franchise — voting rights depended on property, income and education.
  • Reservation: Non-Brahmins in Madras got reserved representation; Depressed Classes were given nominated seats.

Other Provisions & Defects

✓ Other Provisions

  • Provided for the creation of a Public Service Commission.
  • Three out of eight members of the Executive Council were Indians.
  • The office of High Commissioner for India was created in London (1926).

✗ Defects

  • Increased communalism by continuing separate electorates.
  • Indian ministers had little real power; Governors could overrule them at any time.
  • Ministers had no control over finances or senior officials — Diarchy was largely ineffective.

Crown Rule (1858–1947)

Government of India Act, 1935

Est. 1935

The most important constitutional reform before Indian independence — proposed an All-India Federation that never actually came to be.

  • All-India Federation: the Act proposed a federation of British Indian provinces & Princely states. This federation was never actually formed because the princely states did not join.

Division of Powers — Three Lists

ListControlled ByIncluded subjects
Federal ListCentreDefence, foreign affairs, communications
Provincial ListProvincesPolice, agriculture, public health
Concurrent ListCentre & Provinces (shared)Criminal law, marriage, other common subjects

Subjects not mentioned in any list remained under the Governor-General (residual powers).

Provincial Autonomy & Structural Changes

  • Bicameral Legislature in Provinces: introduced in 6 out of the 11 provinces — Madras Presidency, Bombay Presidency, Bengal Presidency, United Provinces, Bihar & Assam. Each had a Legislative Assembly & Legislative Council.
  • Provincial Autonomy: provinces received greater powers and could function more independently. Provincial governments became responsible to the provincial legislatures.
  • Diarchy was abolished at the provincial level and replaced by "monarchy" (Governor's supremacy) at the national level.
  • The Indian Council was abolished, and a Federal Court was constituted.
  • Burma and Aden were cut off from the rest of India.
  • The RBI (Reserve Bank of India) was established as a result of this Act.
  • This Act remained in effect till the new Indian Constitution took its place (1950).
Crown Rule (1858–1947)

Indian Independence Act, 1947

Mountbatten Plan

The final Act — based on the Mountbatten Plan (3 June Plan), it partitioned British India into two independent dominions.

  • The Indian Independence Act was based on the Mountbatten Plan, also known as the 3 June Plan.
  • Attlee's Announcement: Clement Attlee, the British Prime Minister, announced on 20 February 1947 that Britain would transfer power to India by June 1948.

Mountbatten Plan / 3 June Plan

  • The plan was prepared by Lord Louis Mountbatten.
  • It was announced on 3 June 1947.
  • The leaders of the Indian National Congress and the All-India Muslim League accepted the plan.

Main Proposals of the Plan

  • India would be divided into two dominions: India & Pakistan.
  • Independence would be granted from 15 August 1947.
  • A Boundary Commission would determine the new boundaries of India and Pakistan — headed by Sir Cyril Radcliffe.

End of an Era

1773 Regulating Act → ... → 1858 Crown Rule begins → ... → 1947 Indian Independence Act → India & Pakistan become independent dominions on 15 August 1947, closing 174 years of Company & Crown rule.

  • Members were elected indirectly by the Provincial Assemblies through proportional representation with the single transferable vote.

The Indian Constitution is called a "Bag of Borrowings" — features borrowed from the British, American, Irish, Canadian & other world Constitutions.

Impact of the Indian Independence Act, 1947

  • The Constituent Assembly became a fully sovereign body and could alter or repeal any law.
  • The Assembly performed two functions: Constituent body chaired by Dr. Rajendra Prasad, and Legislative body chaired by G.V. Mavalankar.
  • It functioned as the first Parliament of free India. These dual functions continued till 26 November 1949.
  • After the withdrawal of Muslim League members, strength reduced from 389 to 299.

  • National Song: 'Vande Mataram' adopted on 24 January 1950.
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad elected as first President of India on 24 January 1950.
  • National Emblem adopted on 26 January 1950.

  • The Constitution is the supreme law of India.
  • Adopted on 26 November 1949.

  • 26 January is celebrated as Republic Day. 'Republic' means an elected head of the state.

Salient Features of the Constitution of India

Since 26 Jan 1950

The world's longest written constitution, "ransacked" from constitutions across the globe, and balanced — deliberately — between federal form and unitary spirit.

1. Lengthiest Written Constitution

  • Constitutions are of two types: Written Constitution & Unwritten Constitution.
  • United States has the first modern written Constitution.
  • United Kingdom has an unwritten Constitution based on customs and conventions.
  • Indian Constitution is the longest written Constitution in the world.
  • Originally: 395 Articles, 22 Parts & 8 Schedules.
  • At present: 470 Articles, 25 Parts & 12 Schedules.

Reasons for the large / elephantine size of the Constitution

  • Vast and diverse country
  • Influence of the Government of India Act, 1935
  • Single Constitution for Centre and States
  • Presence of legal experts in the Constituent Assembly

2. Drawn from Various Sources

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar said that the Constitution was framed after "ransacking all the known Constitutions of the world."

States & UTs with a Bicameral Legislature

6 States with a Legislative Council: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana & Uttar Pradesh.

2 UTs with a Legislative Assembly-based bicameral setup: Delhi & Puducherry.

5. India Follows a Parliamentary System of Government

  • Based on the British model.
  • Executive responsible to the Legislature.
  • Exists at both Centre and State level.
  • Prime Minister is the real head of government.

6. Constitution Combines

  • British Parliamentary Sovereignty
  • American Judicial Supremacy

7. Rule of Law

  • Law is supreme.
  • No one is above the law.
  • Government is ruled by law, not by individuals.

8. India Has an Integrated and Independent Judiciary

  • Single judicial system for the whole country & the judiciary is independent from the Legislature and Executive.
  • Supreme Court at the top.
  • High Courts in the States.
  • Lower Courts below the High Courts.

9–13. Rights, Duties & Secularism

  • Constitution guarantees 6 Fundamental Rights under Part III.
  • Fundamental Rights protect the liberty and equality of citizens.
  • Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV): aim at social & economic justice, help in creating a welfare state, and are not enforceable by courts.
  • Fundamental Duties: added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976, based on Swaran Singh Committee recommendations. Initially 10 duties; now 11 after the 86th Amendment Act, 2002.
  • Indian Constitution is Secular: no official religion of the State; equal respect and protection to all religions.

14–18. Citizenship, Independent Bodies, Emergency & Local Government

  • India has Single Citizenship: citizens are only citizens of India, not of any state.
  • Constitution provides for Independent Bodies such as: Election Commission, UPSC & CAG.
  • Emergency Provisions: Centre gets more powers during emergency; State governments come under the Centre's control.
  • Three-Tier Government: Centre, State & Local Government.
  • Local Government added by: 73rd Amendment → Panchayats; 74th Amendment → Municipalities. Cooperative Societies got constitutional status through the 97th Amendment Act, 2011.
  • Three organs of Government: Legislature, Executive & Judiciary.

The Preamble

26 Nov 1949

The introduction, preface and summary of the Constitution — its soul distilled into a single, sworn paragraph.

THE CONSTITUTION OF INDIA

Preamble

WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens:

JUSTICE, social, economic and political;

LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship;

EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all

FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation;

IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION.

Words in amber were inserted by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976.

What the Preamble means

  • The term 'Preamble' refers to the introduction or preface to the Constitution — a kind of summary or essence of the whole document.
  • The American Constitution was the first in the world to begin with a preamble; India's own is modelled on this idea.
  • N.A. Palkiwala famously termed the Preamble as "the identity card of the Constitution."
  • K.M. Munshi (Kanhaiyalal Manicklal Munshi) described it as the "political horoscope (kundli) of the Constitution."
  • The Preamble is somewhat based on the 'Objectives Resolution' — moved by Jawaharlal Nehru in the Constituent Assembly in December 1946, laying down the fundamental goals of the future Constitution.

Four Components the Preamble Reveals

Source of Authority

The Constitution derives its authority from the people of India, not any monarch or external power.

Nature of Indian State

Declares India a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic.

Objectives

To secure Justice, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity for every citizen.

Date of Adoption

Adopted on 26th November, 1949 by the Constituent Assembly.

Nature of the Indian State

  • Sovereign — supreme authority; India is free from external control.
  • Democracy — the people rule, either directly or through elected representatives.

Direct Democracy

People participate directly in policy-making — feasible only in low-population areas. Example: Switzerland.

Indirect Democracy

People elect representatives who frame policy on their behalf. Example: India.

The Secular Story

States, classified by how they treat religion:

🚫
Atheistic

No religion allowed at all. E.g. North Korea.

☪️
Theocratic

One state religion. E.g. Pakistan, Bangladesh & Afghanistan (Islamic states); Bhutan & Sri Lanka (Buddhist states).

☸️
Secular

All religions equally respected. E.g. India.

Why "Secular" was added

Part IV (DPSP) gives our Constitution a Socialist character, while Articles 25–28 (Fundamental Rights) give it a Secular character. Hence "Secular" — missing from the original 1949 text — was inserted into the Preamble in 1976.

The Socialist Story

  • Economic systems of the world: Capitalist vs Socialist (which splits into Marxist Socialism and Gandhian Socialism).
  • India is a blend of Capitalism and Socialism.
  • India is also a blend of Marxist and Gandhian Socialism, with greater inclination towards Gandhian socialism — as held in D.S. Nakara vs Union of India.
  • This gave rise to a new term: "Democratic Socialism."
  • Hence "Socialist" — again missing from the original 1949 Preamble — was added by the 1976 amendment.

The Preamble has been amended only once so far — through the 42nd Amendment Act of 1976, which also reshaped several other parts of the Constitution.

⚖ Berubari Union case (1960)

  • The Supreme Court held that the Preamble is NOT a part of the Constitution.

📜 Kesavananda Bharati (1973) & LIC of India (1995)

  • Kesavananda Bharati: SC rejected its earlier opinion and held that the Preamble IS a part of the Constitution — basis of the Basic Structure Doctrine.

Note

The Preamble is neither a source of power to the legislature, nor a prohibition on its powers. Its provisions are non-enforceable in a court of law — that is, it is non-justiciable.

The Seventh Schedule — Union, State & Concurrent Lists

Union List (List I)

Only Parliament can legislate
  • Subjects of national importance
  • Defence (Army, Navy, Air Force)
  • Foreign Affairs, Banking (RBI), Railways, Atomic Energy
  • Lotteries organised by Centre/States (Entry 40)

State List (List II)

Only State Legislatures can legislate
  • Subjects of local/state importance
  • Police & Public Order, Prisons
  • Agriculture, Public Health
  • Local Government, Markets & Fairs

Concurrent List (List III)

Both Parliament & States can legislate
  • Subjects of common interest — originally 47 subjects
  • Education, Forests, Criminal Law
  • Marriage & Divorce
  • Trade Unions
  • Parliament also has authority over Residuary Subjects — matters not included in any of the three lists.
  • Even for a State List subject, Parliament can legislate for any territory of India that is not part of a state.
  • Priority order in case of conflict: Union List > Concurrent List > State List.
  • If Union law and State law conflict on a Concurrent List subject, the Union law prevails — except where the State law was reserved for and received the President's assent, in which case it prevails within that state (Parliament may still override it later by a fresh law).

Eighth Schedule — the 22 Recognised Languages

Originally 14 languages at commencement; now 22, via Articles 344(1) and 351.
